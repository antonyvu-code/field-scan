DESIGN LOG · DECISIONS

Why the portfolio is a radar console.

FIELD SCAN turns a real morning habit — scanning the web for design and engineering signal — into something you play instead of read. This is the reasoning behind it: what problem it solves, why it looks the way it does, and how it keeps itself honest.

▶ OPEN THE LIVE DEMO

01

The premise

Frontend portfolios fall into two traps: the gallery — a grid of thumbnails a hiring manager skims in eight seconds — or the tech-flex — heavy WebGL that impresses for a moment but says nothing about the person. FIELD SCAN avoids both by making the page itself the evidence: it is simultaneously the story of how I work and a working demo of how I build.

Two audiences read it two ways: a hiring lead gets the story in 20 seconds without playing; a senior engineer can toggle INSPECT and read the architecture notes right on the page.

02

A new dialect: SIGNAL

The visual language is a deliberate system, not decoration. Absolute near-black #0A0B0A, a single saturated phosphor green #39FF8F reserved only for live state, a radar coordinate grid as the substrate, and one rule: the content hides in the dark and only exists where you sweep light across it. Information is everywhere; the value is in the act of actively looking — the page's whole argument, made physical.

Two type roles, no third: a serif display (Instrument Serif) for headings and log prose, mono (Space Mono) for every label, number, and HUD readout — mono is the raw material of the dialect, so it appears freely.

03

Why Canvas 2D, not WebGL

A radar sweep over seven blips is O(n) with n = 7. Canvas 2D renders it comfortably at 60fps on any machine, is trivial to debug, and ships in a fraction of the bytes. Reaching for WebGL here would be over-engineering — and knowing when not to reach for the GPU is itself the senior signal. The state model is one pointer object plus a single lockedIndex, and the DOM is written only when the locked signal actually changes — never once per frame.

Motion is delta-time based, so the sweep runs the same speed at 60fps or 144fps; a ResizeObserver sizes the canvas the moment layout is ready and on every resize, and prefers-reduced-motion stops the idle auto-sweep entirely.

04

The numbers are real

Every figure on the page is measured, not invented. The clock is real local time. Signal strength is 1 − clamp(distance / radius), computed live from the cursor. The FPS meter reads true delta time. The DAY count and the signals themselves come from an actual daily digest: a build step parses today's markdown, verifies that every link on the page really appears in it — failing the build if one doesn't — and regenerates the accessible list, the DAY label, and the date from that single source.

That validation is the point: the page claims the links are real, and the toolchain proves it on every build rather than trusting the author.

05

Accessibility on a non-standard interface

A canvas game is invisible to a screen reader, so the scanner has an equivalent reading. The signals sit in a real <ul> of links that works with zero pointer events; the HUD is an aria-live region; there is a skip link, visible focus rings, landmark structure, and contrast that keeps phosphor green for large labels and numbers only — body text stays on the high-contrast ink color.

06

What I'd build next

The pipeline is designed to grow: the digest parser can widen into a fully self-updating feed, the lock blips already hint at a richer Web Audio layer, and a nightly job could redeploy the page each morning so the portfolio is never more than a day stale — a portfolio that literally keeps scanning.

The page is a demo of the ritual. The real thing runs every morning.
